In my youth, I once loved someone, though I never dared to speak of it fully. Because of that love, even the grass, trees, clouds, and evening colors felt dear to me. Later, your face and voice grew distant, while the world remained fresh. Whenever I came upon a lovely scene, delight was followed by a sense of loss. I wanted to forget the sadness, yet treasured the happiness; so I let both come and go within me, and stopped trying to drive them away. Now the gardenia blooms again, and its clear fragrance enters through the window. Remembering those days, I find much that I could write, though I can no longer recall all the words I wanted to say then. Only the flowers we never saw together and the paths we never walked still seem close before my eyes. I gather these scattered threads into this piece, to keep a little sunlight and preserve a trace of fragrance. Thus I write:

It was early summer; the night's rain had just ceased. Gardenias began to bloom, and green shade filled the building. In the morning there were only a few points of white; by noon their fragrance filled the room. Leaves held the fresh moisture, and dew gathered at their tips, poised to fall yet still clinging, swaying lightly in the sun. Voices along the street came through the trees; distant trains followed their tracks, drawing their shadows beneath the clouds. Laughter had just passed outside the building when swallows returned to the eaves. Looking upon that brightness, I felt so at ease that I believed flowers could always bloom, days could always stay, and all that one waited for would be answered.

We were young then, and knew little of passing time. The world within my heart was vast; the days ahead seemed long. Gold flowed through the dusk, and I still thought daylight had not ended. The evening wind carried a tune, and its lingering notes seemed impossible to forget. Finding shade along a path, I wanted to walk slowly and never turn back. Seeing color gather in a cloud, I wished to look up at it together. Had anyone asked what pleased me, I could not have explained it well. There was simply someone to think of, and even the most idle hours suddenly had somewhere to go.

Whenever I thought of you, the world arrived before your face and voice. Sometimes it was the sparse shadows on a window, with the slanting sun moving slowly across them; sometimes the fresh scent among oranges, with a light mist touching my clothes. Before rain, the trees and grass smelled faintly bitter; on a newly clear evening, buildings shone. You stood among these things with your usual expression. You needed neither the moon to lend you its beauty nor flowers to adorn you. Yet I felt the light and shadows around you linger a little at your side. When I tried to understand why, I found nothing to explain it. I only remembered that one glance had made my heart glad.

This feeling was so slight that it could not disturb the long summer. It hid between words and sometimes showed in my eyes. I wished to draw near, then stopped; whatever appeared was quickly withdrawn. You might not have noticed, and I said nothing. Whenever evening colors rose in the sky, my first wish was to tell you. Then I thought: this clear sunset belongs to everyone; why ask you alone whether you have seen it? So I let the thought rest. After the colors faded, I remained at the window a while, allowing dusk to deepen and lights to come on. That small happiness in my heart, though, was slow to settle.

From then on, I saw things in finer detail. Newly opened plane-tree leaves let light through their thin parts; the soft down on their backs shone like gold. Rain followed the curves of the railing, gathering and falling; as one drop was spent, another formed. Late at night, a light still burned in a distant building. Suspended in the silence, it made me feel that someone had a home there too. Things I had once hurried past could now make me stop; things I had called ordinary were now worth attending to. I feared letting a day end too hastily and failing to see it clearly, so that when I told you about it later, it would be missing some of its colors.

Yet most of these words never reached you. They gathered within me, each belonging to its own moment. Before rain, they belonged to the wind turning leaves; at dusk, to the light reflected in windows. There were also things I wanted to say but could not name: neither sorrow nor joy, seeming full and then empty. Sitting quietly for a long time, I almost imagined the green fruit on a branch, in a place where no one spoke, growing sweeter little by little. Now that fruit has ripened and fallen, while the words of those days have still not left my mouth.

I also made many secret plans for days that had not arrived. I wished to follow the flowers until their season was nearly over; to have an unhurried conversation and leave something unfinished for the next morning. If we met at a crossroads, we could walk a few steps together; if there was still light, why not sit a little longer? Without asking what you wanted, I had already reserved a place for you in my future. Thinking of it now, I find it a little absurd, like laying out two cups before inviting a guest. All I wanted then was to say something casually and hear an answer from someone beside me. Such ordinary wishes—and yet I set each one aside without ever telling you.

So the long summer stretched on, and clear shade moved gently. Cicadas called high in the trees, and clouds passed over rows of buildings. White clothes hung in lines in the wind, stirring as though about to take flight. Good things were happening everywhere: young leaves grew into shade, letters from afar entered homes, and travelers newly arrived in strange places found streets they could love. I thought that wherever you went, there would be clear skies of your own, and that what you saw and heard would gradually move beyond my knowing. At that thought, my heart paused for a moment. Then I looked again at the sunlight on the branches, still wishing brightness would remain, so that you might encounter fewer sudden showers along your way.

When speaking of the future, I gradually lost the courage to mention your name lightly. At first I stopped just before my words reached you; later I drew back as soon as the thought arose. I tried to find the point where our paths had separated, but could name no single day. Green fruit lit the branches then, and evening light passed through the buildings. Windows along the street reflected flecks of gold. The world looked lovelier than before, yet we were already growing distant. I postponed what I wanted to say for one day, then another, until there was no way to begin. It was like an old letter carried close to the heart through years of sun and rain: its folds had deepened, yet when opened, its first line still read early summer.

In the old days, when we met, there was often some unfinished conversation at parting. I always thought we could continue it another day. So I left words for tomorrow and scenes for another look. How could I have known that tomorrow would arrive again and again, while the old conversation would never be finished? Whenever I recall a farewell, I feel that moment was too brief. Yet on looking more closely, I see that the light was unhurried and the trees and grass were at peace. Nothing at all had urged us to go.

Ah, how could I become calm so quickly? Sometimes I hoped the wind would return along the old path, and that by chance you might remember the quiet person beneath the trees. One thought had scarcely faded before another arose. I found them pointless, yet could not bear to extinguish them all. Rebuking them too severely only made them more troubled; indulging them too long made me fear hurting myself. So I let them come for a while, and let them leave on their own. The water in my cup cooled, the sound of traffic receded, and the slight warmth in my heart settled with them. The next morning, I opened the window. The gardenias were still white, untouched by my restless night.

It is only when a beautiful scene lies before me that I find my feelings hardest to place. The last red deepens into purple; weary birds cross the sky; layer after layer of dusk unfolds, lovelier than anything I once expected. Delighted, I want to offer it to someone, but you are no longer where I can call to you freely. Words form in my heart and stop again at my lips. Alone, I watch until lights come on in countless homes. I wonder whether you too might have looked up just then and seen the same clear evening. Even if you had, I would have no way of knowing. As I turn back, my sleeve brushes the corner of the railing and picks up a little dust. I lower my head and brush it away, but a faint mark remains.

There were also days when everything seemed faded. Flowers stood by the window, but I had no wish to distinguish their scents; a melody reached my ears, and I grew tired before it ended. I knew the wind and sunlight had done me no wrong, yet for a while there was no room within me to welcome them. The world was patient enough to leave me to my indolence without hurrying me: rain sounded beneath the eaves when it came, and tree shadows moved when the sky cleared. After some time, I happened to notice a new shoot in a pot, a little taller than the day before, and a small happiness arose again. It came so ordinarily. Sitting and looking at it for a while was enough.

Sometimes I went a whole day without thinking of you. When I noticed, I felt as though I had lost something. You had never asked me to remember you always, and I had never made such a promise. Why, then, should briefly forgetting for a day make me feel faintly guilty? I found the thought rather ridiculous, but after laughing, a little sadness remained. So I got up to water the flowers. The soil had long been dry; as water entered it, the earth slowly softened. Only then did I realize how many things before my eyes still needed tending.

Yet what you left has never entirely departed. To this day I love going out after the rain stops, and looking up at the colors of clouds at dusk. Reading “the spring waters are bluer than the sky,” I often pause at that line, imagining how a river of clear blue could hold all the daylight. Things I once easily overlooked can now linger in my heart. Even loneliness can be listened to closely: insects calling late at night, a curtain falling still when the wind settles, a light going out in a distant building while a few stars remain above. Sometimes I wish to tell you these things. The thought is light; it rises and soon rests again. Only that river of clear blue, renewed by several rains, was never something we could look upon together.

I have also slowly come to understand that you have your own weary hours, your worries, your times of walking down a street without wanting to speak. You may not love the scenes I love, or remember the things I remember. In those days, watching you from afar, I often added brightness of my own imagining, until I almost forgot that you too were an ordinary person. Now, removing some of that ornament and returning you to your own face, I find your presence closer. I imagine you sitting alone, perhaps with a slight concern between your brows that you do not wish to tell anyone; returning at dusk, you might also feel your heart ease a little at a pleasant breeze. Thinking of these things, I still find you dear.

If I could rewrite the old days, I would leave them unfinished. Where there was no answer, I would let the space remain; where there was no promise, I would not force a continuation. Nor need the young person I was then know in advance of the meetings and partings to come. That person still stood beneath the clear shade, believing the long days could wait and distant roads could be walked together. Hearing a single call was enough to bring happiness for a long time. Thinking of that younger self, I suddenly wish to urge him to tell you sooner what he wanted to say. Then I hesitate again, not knowing whether, if he truly spoke, those days could still have stretched on so gently. So I say nothing either, and watch him quietly rejoicing on his own.

As the years deepen, forgetting comes at last. Trying to recall something you said, I often remember your smile but lose the words; seeking an old scene, I know only that it was lovely, and cannot say when it happened. Once I feared my feelings were too deep; now I regret remembering less each day. I only hope a few details will remain: light swaying along a clothesline, scattered sounds between rain shelters, the fresh scent rising when fruit peel first breaks. You and I are both there, not yet hurried by the years to come, each at ease with what we do not know. Though some wishes went unfulfilled, the goodness of that day was something I truly received.

Today a flower has fallen by the window, its petal edges faintly yellow. I pick it up; it is still fragrant. Setting it on the sill, I listen to the voices in the street: still bustling, still coming and going. There are many summers left in my life, and many mornings of newly clear skies. Flowers should turn toward the sun; the window need not remain shut. Only when I happen upon a full bloom do I still save a moment for you, as though waiting for someone beside me to look with me. Soon the wind passes, the flowering branch sways lightly, and that moment passes too.

The wind comes and turns the pages of a book. I reach out to hold them down, suddenly wanting to write every word I left unsaid in those old days. To write how the rain stopped, how the lamps shone, how a young person found happiness alone for no reason at all. As for you, there is no need to invent another extraordinary encounter; you need only appear in ordinary days, just as you did at first. Then the trees fill with flowers and the window with clear light. When I reach the place where I want to invite you to look with me, my pen pauses. I press the fallen flower at the end of the pages and leave a line blank. Its fragrance remains; outside the window, evening is slowly drawing near.
