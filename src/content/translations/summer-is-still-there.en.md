I hate summer.

Most of all, I hate how late darkness comes. It is already past seven, yet a pale wash of gold still rests on the distant rooftops, and the edges of the clouds refuse to dim. The leaves have spent the whole day beneath the sun, but at this hour they somehow find the spirit to turn green again. Someone cycles past; a bell rings softly, and the slope carries him toward a brighter place. Standing by the window, I feel for no particular reason that an evening like this ought to hold one more event.

But the wind only keeps blowing.

The curtain swells and settles back into place. Laughter rises through the branches from downstairs; I can no longer make out the words, but the happiness is unmistakable. Summer is full of this unaccountable joy: the faint crack of a watermelon splitting open, bubbles welling over the rim of a glass, the soft scent of sunlight in clothes just taken in from the line. These things gather so close that refusing them is almost impossible. I reach out too. The cold glass presses against my palm, and the corners of my mouth lift. Only after I smile does my gaze sometimes drift a little to one side.

That small empty place is always brighter than everywhere else.

I used to love summer. I loved its lack of restraint: flowers reaching beyond the wall, grass growing into cracks between the bricks, rain refusing to fall properly and insisting on drenching half the city. Even the clouds came in great masses, crowded into the sky and so white that the days seemed impossible to use up. A young heart cannot withstand weather like that. One newly green tree was enough to make me imagine an entire street; one name was enough to make me feel that all the summers ahead now belonged to someone with whom they could be discussed.

Back then, even “afterward” was a light word, held in the mouth like a peppermint that had not yet dissolved.

Summer is also accustomed to hiding things for people. A blush can be blamed on the heat; silence, on the cicadas being too loud; lingering too long, on the evening breeze being pleasant. At the time, I wished every feeling could have such an easy excuse. I wanted you to see through me a little, but never completely. Keeping a person's name at the edge of the lips required more care than holding an ice cube in the mouth. Ice melts and water is swallowed, but that name remains, sometimes brushing gently against teeth and lips with each breath, making me shy before anything has even happened.

Looking back, we did not really say much about the future. I was the one who took the unfinished sentences and quietly continued them too far. You said the sky was blue, and I added a distant sea to that blue. You said the summer holiday was long, and I waited for the wind at the end of August as well. Those imaginings were so quiet that they never disturbed you, yet here they had already grown into branches and leaves. When it was time to take them back, I did not know where to begin. So I let them keep growing for a while, like an unnamed plant in the corner of a balcony—untouched, yet greener every day.

Summer knew nothing of this.

In the morning, sunlight still passes through the gaps in the leaves and scatters small round patches across the white wall. A few sparrows quarrel over a scrap of shade, then sit side by side on a power line when the quarrel is done. The glass door of the convenience store opens and closes, letting cold air drift briefly across the threshold. A girl in a white dress runs into the sunlight carrying a bag of ice pops. Someone sells gardenias by the street; drops of water still cling to the tips of their thick leaves, but their scent has already wandered away and turned the corner. The whole city looks freshly washed—young, clear, and busy beginning another day.

It is difficult to be sincerely sad on a morning like this. Even melancholy feels unreasonable, as though someone has polished the window perfectly clean and I insist on leaving a patch of mist upon it. Yet the mist is very thin. Sometimes it comes from hearing a familiar song; sometimes from recognizing a similar way of walking in a crowd. Something stirs faintly inside me, like wind passing over a string far away. Before I can hear the lingering note, the light at the intersection has already turned green.

I keep walking, and I know that person is not you.

The afternoon gradually grows hot, and a trembling layer of light floats above the asphalt. The roadside trees are so dense their leaves look almost black; the cicadas call from within them, one cry after another, as if they never need to breathe. “In the deep shade of green trees, the summer day is long.” Once, I read that line and saw only a lovely scene. Now I understand the word “long.” Ice knocks against the bottom of my glass several times. I turn a few pages of a book and turn them back again, while the shadow of the sun moves no more than an inch. An afternoon is enough time to carry thoughts that should no longer be thought very far away, then pretend I was only daydreaming for a while.

You probably do not know that my longing often looks nothing like longing. It has nothing it must say. I simply see a beautiful cloud, and before thought catches up, my finger has opened that familiar profile. By the time I understand what I have done, the screen is glowing, the message box is empty, and the clouds above are still changing. What could I say? That a cloud here looks like a sail, or that the wind has finally become a little cooler? Once, such small things required no deliberation. The moment I begin to deliberate, not one sentence seems fit to begin with.

So I put my phone down. The cloud disperses faster than I expected; the sail loosens and becomes a boundless whiteness again. There is nothing worth regretting—summer never lacks clouds. Yet when the next beautiful cloud drifts by, you are still the first person I think of.

Sometimes I also hope for rain. The sky darkens, leaves turn their grey-white undersides to the wind, the clothesline snaps and rattles, and footsteps in the street suddenly quicken. At such times, all those directionless thoughts inside me find somewhere to settle. I can sit quietly and do nothing, listening for the first drop to strike the sill. Soon the distant buildings blur, the nearby leaves brighten one by one, and the smell of wet grass and trees rises from the burning dust. The room grows dim, and at last I no longer need to squint.

But summer rain stops too easily. Before the puddles recede, the sun peeks out from behind the clouds and makes every wet trace dazzling. Children have already run away splashing through the water, while drops still fall one by one from the eaves. The soaked world is sharper than before; even the moss at the foot of the wall has found a lovely colour. The little gloom I meant to borrow is washed clean in an instant. Half a rainbow even lies across the horizon, faintly, as though someone made a few strokes on a whim and then hurried off to do something happier.

I look at it and still think it is beautiful.

That annoys me a little. I cannot turn cold toward the whole of summer because of you. The evening wind still feels good against my skin. The first bite of iced watermelon is still so sweet I am reluctant to swallow it. When my headphones suddenly play a song I love, my steps still grow lighter. I am not deeply sad, at least not most of the time. But whenever these good things reach me, they arrive with an old habit: the wish to share them with you. My hand has already reached out before I remember to lower it slowly to my side.

To have met at all is already the finest fortune; why insist that everything be made complete?

Dusk spreads inch by inch, and summer changes its appearance again. The sky is a deep blue; the streetlights are on, yet beneath the trees people's faces are still clear. Young faces hold both sweat and light. Everyone looks as though they have just run out of a long wait and are hurrying toward some solemn appointment. The wind lifts the hem of a shirt. A white sneaker swings gently from the back of a bicycle. Somewhere in the distance, someone calls another person's name with such ease. The person being called turns around, and before the smile reaches their lips, it is already bright in their eyes.

Sometimes all I envy is the sound of that name being called.

A name is ordinarily such a simple thing: only a few strokes on paper, hardly any space inside a sentence. Yet for a time, it can make so many ordinary things worth anticipating. For several minutes each day, I would pay special attention to whether my phone lit up. A familiar route would gain a reason for taking the long way around because of one tiny possibility. Now those roads have returned to their original lengths. Students pour past after school, streetlights come on one by one, and there is no longer any corner before which I need to straighten my collar.

Perhaps that is for the best. Summer is hot enough already; there is no need to weigh a single sentence over and over until even my palm begins to sweat. Sometimes I think this and almost feel relieved. But when the wind comes and folds my collar against my neck, I still reach up to smooth it down.

The riverside is lively too. Someone plays guitar beneath the bridge, not quite in tune, but plenty of people hum along. The sound travels close to the water, broken into pieces by the evening wind. Reflections from both banks meet in the middle of the river, scatter with the ripples, and gather again, as though a hand could scoop them up. On a night like this, nobody needs to hurry home. The stone steps have been warmed by the people sitting on them. A few fallen leaves lie in the shallows, while petals drifting down from upstream circle at my feet. Sometimes I envy the ease of a single petal: it can stop anywhere it pleases without feeling it has failed such a fine wind and moon.

At night, the city's liveliness is slow to fade. Folding chairs are set outside little shops; newly bought peaches sit inside plastic bags; someone waves from half a street away. Water from an air conditioner falls drop by drop into a flowerpot. Laughter from a television crosses a balcony and joins the faint music farther away. These sounds have nothing to do with me, yet after listening for a while, I find them comforting. I am no longer in a hurry to close the window, and let them remain in the room for a time. Jasmine must have opened on someone's balcony. Its scent comes strong, then faint, so close it seems the flowers have been placed on my own sill.

When the moon rises, the dense crowns of the daytime trees are reduced to silhouettes. The wind carries off some of the heat. A page turns and rests at a passage that does not matter. At this hour, when I think of you, I no longer always think of us. I also wonder whether there are trees outside your window and whether the night is just as cool where you are. The thought is brief and has no conclusion. This does not make me feel much wiser. If I happened to hear news of you, my heart would probably still be unsettled for a while; if we truly met, I might not be as natural as I imagine.

Gazing at the same moon yet unable to hear one another, I would follow its light to shine upon you. The wild geese fly on, but cannot carry the moonlight beyond; fish and dragons leap beneath the water, stirring ripples into patterns.

Summer is still long. These things can take their time.

I am not in any hurry to forgive it. It leaves dusk so bright and makes the wind so gentle that many hopes already come to nothing occasionally begin to grow again at the wrong moment. Yet I cannot bear to urge it away. In a few more days, the sounds in the trees will thin. The sleeping mat will be stored in the cupboard, short-sleeved shirts washed and folded, and the evening sky will darken before anyone remembers to look up. Then, even if I want to sit idly by the window, I will no longer have such a long stretch of light.

At this moment, someone in the distance begins setting off fireworks again. Several rows of buildings stand between us, so only the higher bursts are visible—red, gold, opening one after another across the night sky. Someone downstairs cries out in delight. More people push open their windows and lift their faces. The delayed sound makes the glass tremble slightly. I watch too, waiting for the next flower of light to rise. The phone in my palm is silent. The sky is wide enough; if you happen to look up, perhaps you can see it as well.

This feeling might have become a memory, only I was already lost in it at the time.

Next year, summer will still arrive as promised.
