The college entrance exam is actually a Soulslike game: you have to grind questions and memorize the patterns.

Finding a job is a Soulslike too. There is very little room for error, and it is a long walk just to reach the boss.

Marriage is another Soulslike: first you have to finish a huge pile of prerequisite side quests.

Going to work is even more Soulslike. You rest at the bonfire every day, and the enemies respawn the next morning.

Looked at this way, life is one enormous Soulslike game.

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

First, step aside, “Big Water Theory.”

The Soulslike genre originated with FromSoftware's Demon's Souls and Dark Souls series. Its core can be summed up in three words: asking for punishment.

Almost nonexistent tutorials, NPCs who never stop speaking in riddles, storytelling fragmented beyond recognition, and, most importantly, extremely difficult combat and severe penalties for dying.

The suffering also includes, but is not limited to: doors that do not open from this side; a very long walk before every boss attempt; treasure chests that turn out to be traps; bosses reading your attack inputs; and attacks with infuriatingly delayed timing.

<mark class="theme-highlight">If you want to know whether Soulslikes are for you, slap yourself. If it hurts, they are not. If you enjoy it, congratulations: you are already a seasoned Souls player.</mark>

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

Recently, a new stock comment on the internet has attached itself to this relatively niche corner of gaming: “Another Soulslike.”

At first it appeared only beneath gaming posts. Later, it gradually spread to completely unrelated videos, where someone would inexplicably chime in: “Ah, another Soulslike.”

An entrance-exam math paper? Another Soulslike. Both parents coming at you? “Another Soulslike.” A child is born? Another Soulslike. Genshin Impact is a Soulslike, Legends of the Three Kingdoms is a Soulslike, and Honor of Kings is a Soulslike too. Trading stocks? Also a Soulslike.

Even this video of mine that you are watching right now is a Soulslike.

Hidetaka Miyazaki, just how far can those eyes of yours see?

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

These comparisons are hilariously far-fetched and difficult to keep a straight face over. But on closer inspection, their popularity does have a certain logic behind it.

<mark class="theme-highlight">High difficulty and little tolerance for error are the most obvious features of a Soulslike. Coincidentally, they are also the defining conditions of life for most ordinary people, apart from a small group at the very top of the social hierarchy.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">If, as an ordinary person, you want a relatively successful life by conventional standards and a perfect ending, your Soulslike begins here.</mark>

School entrance exams, the senior-high entrance exam, the college entrance exam: you keep leveling up, but the further you go, the less room there is for error. The bosses get harder, and their moves become more elaborate.

After graduation, side quests open up for civil-service exams, public-sector recruitment and postgraduate study. Choose one and the other quest lines close. These quests are not merely difficult: you also have to make sure the one you are pursuing can trigger the ending you want. The uncertainty is enormous, and there is no reliable walkthrough.

Want to find a good job? Open a recruitment app and it tells you: “You don't have the right. You don't have the right.”

Try to take a shortcut now and then, and you run into the rule that “the door does not open from this side.” All you can do is carry on the hard way, one little enemy at a time.

Meanwhile, you must constantly watch out for people hiding in the shadows who could ambush you and ruin your entire life.

Throughout this process, you fail again and again, getting knocked down and getting back up. In the end, only a very small chance remains of reaching the perfect ending you wanted.

Good grief. That sounds even more Soulslike than a Soulslike.

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

At this point you may be thinking: so “the great Soulslike” just means life is one enormous Soulslike game? I could have learned that from the title. Why did I click on your article? There go another few minutes of my life.

<mark class="theme-highlight">But what I want to say is that life is a Soulslike game—and yet that is not entirely true.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">Beyond the familiar and most obvious features of high difficulty and low tolerance for error, Soulslikes make other balancing choices that many people overlook.</mark>

Suppose you are Hidetaka Miyazaki. When you design a game, is your purpose simply to torment players and make them suffer?

Of course that cannot be the only point. Otherwise all the players would leave, and <mark class="theme-highlight">what would I live on?</mark>

Your aim should be to make players feel that the suffering is worthwhile. In other words, even at a very high difficulty, players need positive feedback along the way.

That is why Soulslikes, whether made by FromSoftware or other studios, have two additional features that are easy to overlook.

First, although bosses are difficult, their moves do not change at random. If you cannot beat them in one or two attempts, you can memorize their patterns and try again.

“However long they want to fight, we will fight for however long it takes, until complete victory.”

With enough patience, beating the boss is only a matter of time.

There was a 43-year-old man who challenged himself to finish Elden Ring without reading guides, changing weapons, leveling up, or using incantations or Spirit Ashes. It ultimately took him 8,853 attempts to beat Malenia and 9,868 to beat the final boss, Radagon.

So even when Souls games are difficult, they still make the rules visible and the outcome highly predictable. After all, you can die countless times, but the boss only has to die once.

Second, every Souls player chooses to enter this suffering voluntarily. Whether to continue is also a matter of personal choice.

If it stops being fun or becomes too irritating, you can uninstall and quit. If you have played for less than two hours, you can even request a refund. If you are still annoyed, you can go online and post stories about Miyazaki's childhood to vent your frustration.

Playing this game, or choosing not to play it, is voluntary. Players are also free to express themselves in community discussions.

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<mark class="theme-highlight">Yet it is not hard to see that reality inherits only the high difficulty and low tolerance for error, without making concessions to balance. Reality does not care whether you receive positive feedback.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">It cannot guarantee complete fairness or transparent rules, and everyone starts somewhere different. Some people begin with a fully loaded cheat menu before they even choose a class; others begin as a Wretch.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">At opportunities such as major exams and job applications, you do not get endless chances to learn from mistakes. Once an opportunity is missed, it often stays missed, with no extra attempt waiting for you.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">Reality cannot guarantee that you joined voluntarily, and leaving voluntarily is hardly straightforward either. You cannot simply exit the biosphere, can you?</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">Life therefore inherits the most obvious outward feature of a Soulslike—its difficulty—without the corresponding balancing adjustments. It looks like a Soulslike, but it is a hollow imitation that has the form without the substance.</mark>

“The great Soulslike” does not simply mean that life equals a Soulslike. It means that when an environment combines opaque rules, extremely little room for error, a single path to success, harsh punishment, delayed rewards and the attribution of every failure to individual performance, people are compelled to experience life as a Soulslike. That is how they try to make sense of suffering that seems to have no reason, and reach some inner reconciliation.

<mark class="theme-highlight">This is fundamentally determined by the game environment, rather than by the player's own performance.</mark>

Return to the opening test: slap yourself, and only those who enjoy it count as Souls players. Here is another thing Soulslikes and life have in common: the language of game difficulty replaces the experience of suffering.

The idea is: “A little hardship is perfectly normal. That's just the difficulty of the game. You have to endure the bitterest hardship to rise above everyone else.”

As in a Souls game, we accept the idea that “the difficulty is this high, so suffering a little is only natural.” The question in our minds changes from “is it reasonable to put this suffering here?” to “how do I clear this obstacle?”

We start studying guides and analyzing our deaths. In real life, that means competing over qualifications, résumés and experience, grinding ourselves into the ground. Very few people stop to ask: why was this game designed to be so difficult in the first place?

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<mark class="theme-highlight">The answer is simple: in reality, this difficulty is not designed to serve you.</mark>

First, whom does a Soulslike serve? Obviously, the player. Miyazaki makes it difficult so that winning feels good. A sense of achievement is the game's real selling point and the player's real reason for playing. Difficulty is only a means of attaining that feeling.

In reality, however, the Soulslike system actually serves a mechanism of selection.

Imagine you work in HR and face hundreds of résumés. You cannot investigate every person's actual ability one by one; the cost is too high. What do you do? You filter out social labels such as “not from a prestigious university” to reduce the workload.

<mark class="theme-highlight">For applicants, however, that becomes a system of high difficulty and low tolerance for error.</mark> If you missed your chance earlier and never obtained the high-education item, you hit a progression lock here and can only take a detour.

Education works in a similar way. When the system cannot uncover every student's own talents and abilities, exam-based education becomes the simplest mechanism of selection.

As the saying goes, a thousand troops and ten thousand horses cross a single-log bridge. Good students at key high schools are more likely to enter good universities; good students at key middle schools are more likely to enter key high schools; and good students at key primary schools are more likely to enter key middle schools.

The stages are linked together. Every step is crucial, and none seems to allow a mistake. For students, that is another form of high difficulty and low tolerance for error.

From the perspective of selection, high difficulty and low tolerance for error become especially useful tools: they divide people with maximum efficiency.

<mark class="theme-highlight">A Soulslike is difficult in order to help the player grow; reality is difficult simply in order to separate people.</mark>

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

Does that mean selection is such a complete system, built from layer upon layer of rules, that we can only resign ourselves to following its prescribed route and compete all the way to the end?

Not necessarily.

We may not be able to change the game itself for now, but we can use the Workshop to install our own mods and create an entirely different experience.

What does that mean?

People who often play games on Steam know the Workshop as a place where players can add their own rules within an existing game framework, without touching its underlying engine or seeking official approval.

Take a recent popular example: someone installed a Minecraft mod in Elden Ring. It was not merely a change to character models. It brought an entire Minecraft-style sandbox system into Elden Ring, turning a punishing dark-fantasy world into a sandbox where you can farm, build houses and explore at your own pace.

Sometimes, as players keep adding their own mods, the game becomes less and less like its original form. It may even become a new game altogether.

<mark class="theme-highlight">Take Slay the Spire, which I often play. The first game's base version has only four characters, and the second adds only one more. Yet its long-term life and replayability depend not just on official updates, but even more on all sorts of community-made Slay the Spire mods.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">Anime-girl mods, Hextech mods, cultivation mods, Sakiko Togawa mods—even Elden Ring mods.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">These days, the internet is a murky arena of social PvP, where even posting a casual comment means high difficulty and little tolerance for error. People increasingly feel that every game is fundamentally a Soulslike, so they simply post an irresistibly absurd “another Soulslike.”</mark>

But people in Workshop and mod-development communities may instead feel that every game is fundamentally a sandbox.

“What? Miyazaki has released another game? Another sandbox.”

The same principle applies to reality.

Sociology has a familiar pair of concepts: “structure and agency.” Structure is what we cannot change for the moment, such as the selection mechanism or the difficulty. Agency is the room we still have within that framework to define how we live.

In reality, selection mechanisms are like the game engine and underlying development framework: we cannot change them for now. But above that engine sits a quest list and an achievement system written by the developers:

<mark class="theme-highlight">What level of play counts as success? What counts as completing the whole game? Which ending counts as a good ending?</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">This layer can, in fact, be replaced. It is the Workshop that each of us holds in our own hands.</mark>

Of course, this is not the same as saying “just change your mindset.” The difficulty, mechanics and unreasonable design choices really exist. Pain is pain, and suffering is suffering.

The real meaning of the Workshop is that we can acknowledge the difficulty while no longer treating the developers' predetermined criteria as the only standard for playing this game well.

>
> <mark class="theme-highlight">Perhaps there was never only one road to becoming a lord.</mark>

As in that very popular Elden Ring meme online (which I couldn't find), people my age buy cars, buy homes and start families, while I have merely become the Elden Lord in a game.

Although the comparison is meant as a joke, becoming the Elden Lord in a game is also a pursuit in life, a way of finding reconciliation with yourself, and a unique experience. It is not somehow much less worthy than starting a family and building a career.

Any experience of your life should be allowed to count as a good ending. There is no absolute Happy Ending or Bad Ending.

The road to becoming a lord in reality was never a fixed route with only one choice. It is not meant to be dictated or directed by anyone else.

After all, even the game has already told you: though guidance has long been shattered, please become the Elden Lord.

In the end, Miyazaki leaves players more than the malice that makes them throw their controllers. Beyond a heavy door, there is also the Erdtree suddenly filling your vision, the river of stars, and the wind over the ruins.

This lousy game called reality may not have given us a particularly good engine, and the little everyday enemies will still respawn on schedule tomorrow morning. But once you start installing your own mods, the high walls once used to sort you become scenery you can wander through however you like.

Whether you finally take your seat on that conventional throne is not so important after all. Souls players are good at refusing the prescribed route. In a system that was never particularly fair, completing the journey in a way you like already counts as a top-tier strategy for outwitting the mechanics.

<mark class="theme-highlight">When every golden grace has faded and every established guide has failed, you finally stop being a Tarnished pushed along by a quest list. The wilderness is vast, with no signposts. That means that from this moment on, wherever you choose to go is where your throne stands.</mark>
