The flowers on the hillside were blooming almost excessively.

White blossoms pressed against white blossoms all the way around the back of the mountain. Even in the cracks between the rocks, tiny flowers held up a touch of tender yellow and nodded in the wind. The rain had only just stopped; before the drops on the tips of the leaves could fall, the sun came out and lit the whole hillside with a wet gleam. A bee burrowed into the heart of a flower and bent its stem. After it flew away, the flower was still swaying, as though it had kept the last of an unfinished smile.

Weather like this makes it easy to forget certain things. That a mountain path has an end, for instance, and that spring water will recede; that a name has gone unspoken for so long that, before calling it again, one has to clear one's throat.

I still think you would like it here. In the distance, mountains are so green they turn blue. Nearby are stones where we could sit. The stream winds around them, divides in two, then quietly joins itself again downstream. If you came, there would be no need to speak right away. First, look at that butterfly: each time it tries to land, the wind pushes it a little farther, until it simply flies higher and higher. In another moment, the shadow of a cloud will pass over us.

Perhaps those are all the things I want to tell you. Which leaf is hiding rain on its underside, which flower has a scent you must lean close to find, why the mountain suddenly turns purple at dusk. When I was young, I tried to hide all that my heart could not contain inside small things like these. I thought I had hidden it well, but the moment I saw you, the whole mountain seemed to give me away.

You may never have noticed. When you looked at the flowers, I looked at them too, only a little later. It was only after your gaze had moved elsewhere that I discovered how that branch had bloomed. I was secretly annoyed at such a small delay, yet for many days afterward I remembered a flower I otherwise would have forgotten.

The flowers have bloomed several times since then, but their colors have not faded. The early spring wind first brushes the tips of the grass green, then scatters little flecks of white through the valley; by late spring, everything is so lush the colors seem impossible to thin. I thought memory would grow old with the years, or at least resemble a flower pressed in a book, shedding a few fragments at the lightest touch. Yet when I think of you, the sunlight is still fresh, down to the faint itch of it on my skin.

And of all times, we parted under a sky just as beautiful. The trees and grasses had not lost their enthusiasm, shards of gold danced on the water, and even the wind felt pleasant. After you turned away, a bird called out in the mountains, startlingly clear. I stood there for a while and, for no reason I could name, felt that to be sad just then would be to let such an afternoon go to waste.

So many things went unsaid. The wind came in gusts, filling my sleeves and letting them fall empty again; the seed heads of the grass brushed the back of my hand and tickled. I lowered my head to avoid a stray vine. When I looked up, there was no longer anyone on the path I needed to make room for. The mountain path had widened, and the sun was still high enough for one person to walk slowly home.

Back then, I always thought losing each other would be brief. As long as the path kept winding beneath our feet, it would turn around a ridge and bring us both to the same place again. Streams bend, winds change direction, and even fallen leaves circle a few times in a puddle. After ten thousand winters come ten thousand springs; after ten thousand farewells, ten thousand reunions. The certainty of youth needed no reason. It was as though that little warmth in my chest could shorten the journey and give an ending to things we had never promised.

I am in no hurry to reproach the person I was then. If even he had refused to believe, who would have kept me company through all those unanswered evenings that came later? All he could do was point awkwardly toward the distant mountains and say there was still a road. He had no idea how long it was or how many storms lay along it. He had counted only the finest weather.

Sometimes I wonder what you saw first after we parted. Was it a tree coming into bud, or the pale moon at the edge of the sky? You must soon have found new scenery, some of it better than this, some of it merely unfamiliar but still enough to make you pause. I imagine you lifting your head, and I feel a little happiness. Then I realize that happiness has nowhere to go.

If only I could hand it to you, the way one offers a freshly washed green fruit, water still clinging to one's fingers, without having to explain why. But green fruit ripens, and ripe fruit falls. By the time I had finally worked out how to speak, the leaves on that tree had grown too deep to see any fruit at all.

At night, the hillside gradually seemed to flatten. The flowers whose distances were clear by day merged into a pale wash beneath the moon, while the sound of the stream drew closer. Stars appeared one after another. After watching them for a long time, I could no longer tell whether they were near the earth or the darkness had lifted the mountain toward them.

I like to imagine two of those stars moving slowly along their own paths. For now, a vast stretch of deep blue lies between them: one is about to sink, while the other has only just risen above the summit. The rivers and open fields they illuminate must be very different. But the sky is so wide that it must allow for one long detour. After the plants on earth have changed color several times, might their two paths pass one night above the same sea of flowers?

I know it is a childish thought. Stars cannot make promises for people. After sitting there so long, I feel dew dampen the toes of my shoes and the cold inside the stone slowly rise through me. An insect occasionally calls in the distance. My neck aches from looking up, and at last I lower my head, but I still cannot bring myself to leave at once, as though waiting a little longer might let me see that almost imperceptible movement.

Many nights passed like that. When day came, the mountain was still a mountain. A few petals had fallen; one clung to the mud by the water, translucent and so thin it seemed about to vanish. I brushed it lightly with a fingertip but could not lift it. Water flowed past and brought a new blade of grass. Only then did I begin to fear: if our reunion comes too late, will we still recognize each other?

Perhaps you have formed new habits. Perhaps you no longer like the colors you once did, and the same cloud no longer brings you the same thought. The years will have left things I know nothing of in the pauses of your speech. You will mention them and casually name unfamiliar places, then smile as though they were the most ordinary experiences. I will have to listen carefully to keep up with all the years you have traveled without me.

I will change too. Many details I cannot bear to lose now may not remain clear forever. One day I may need a familiar gust of wind just to remember your voice. I used to resist the thought, as though each thing forgotten took away a little of my right to see you again. Yet on the nights when I finally slept peacefully, you were absent from my dreams, and when I woke, my heart was not empty.

There was another thing I used to fear. One day you might meet someone better suited to you, and the place I occupied in your memory would have to shift aside. Later I came to think that even that place was probably one I had assigned to myself. You never promised me anything. Whenever I remember the hand you held out to me at the start of our journey, Teyvat seems to settle across the lake in my heart. I remember the warmth of your palm. As for reaching the end together, that was a step I imagined for myself when I took your hand. Now I quietly take that step back. The ground feels a little hollow beneath me, but I can still stand.

Spring still makes me happy. When new leaves first unfold, their green is so tender it seems wrong to touch them. The earth after rain has a faintly bitter smell. Sometimes I stand beneath a tree and listen to the wind turn the leaves in layer after layer, and find I am willing to stay there for a long time.

I still keep a little place for you in my heart. It is small; it does not prevent things from growing, or keep me from moving forward. Only when the flowers are especially beautiful do I think how lovely it would be if you happened to pass this way. The sea of flowers waits for the one blossom that left to return, waits for the day when hearts and petals grow soft. Such thoughts arrive lightly and fall lightly, like a petal resting on my shoulder until, far down the road, a passing wind carries it away.

But I can never quite picture the day we meet again. I tend to imagine fine weather, though rain would do just as well. We could stand beneath the eaves and listen to drops falling from above; perhaps words would come more easily then. It turns out there is no need to ask everything on the same day. If a long separation has made us strangers, we can begin by talking about the weather and the tree we can see before us.

What I fear is rehearsing our meeting so many times that, when it finally happens, I will recognize only the person I imagined. So I try to let go of all the words I prepared long ago. When you come, you may be silent. You may be quite different from who you used to be. You may simply want to rest a while in the shade. I will wait for you to tell me what you like these days and where you hope to go next. The stories I have never heard can be told slowly.

Perhaps halfway through one of them, you will suddenly laugh. For a moment I will be startled; then I will laugh too. The unfamiliar years will still be there, but that smile will make me feel we can look at the things before us together again. The sound of water, the shadows of trees, and the sunlight falling through the leaves will all be here, now; we will not have to search memory for them. Perhaps the past cannot be revisited, but there is still a tomorrow when I might meet you again.

At that thought, it almost seems a road has lit up in the distance. I still do not know where it leads. I cannot say how the sun and rain will alternate from here, or in what season I might reach it. There is water in the mountains and there are trees beside the road, and there will always be places worth stopping along the way. I can walk at an easy pace. If mud sticks to my shoes, I can wipe it off on the grass; when I am tired, I can sit on a dry stone.

We will meet again. When I think those words, I suddenly want to cup my hands around my mouth and call them toward the mountain opposite. But across the valley a bird calls first, and another answers from farther away, their voices thin and distant. Listening, I forget what I had meant to do. After a while, I find myself smiling instead. The wind comes from the far bank, and the grasses bow one after another, all the way to my feet.

The wind bends another patch of white flowers before me. Bees hum busily, the stream curves around the stones, and the sun has already crossed the summit. When I open my hand, I find a little pollen on my palm. I must have touched a flower earlier and forgotten. I try to brush it off, only to spread it more evenly across my fingers: a faint layer of yellow.

You see, this is how spring sometimes persuades a person to stay. I meant only to pass through, yet I carry the scent of plants all over me and, even after walking a long way, cannot help looking back. Of course the flowers are still blooming, bright and lively; they do not spare a single color because someone is gone. I have grown rather fond of that temperament.

We will meet again.

I have said it softly, and that is enough to make it count. I will ask the open country for no echo. The mountain wind blows, and all the white flowers on the slope lean away together, revealing a narrow path beneath them. I bend to push aside a branch, and its fragrance catches on my sleeve. I can still smell it after I have walked a long way.

If you arrive late and the flowers on the hillside have already faded, do not be too sorry. Follow the stream uphill. In the shade, a few are still blooming late, with cool dew on their petals. When you reach them, call my name once more.

Softly is enough. I might be bent over a flower then, my sleeve touching the dew. When you call, I will answer at once, as though you had merely gone down to the stream to wash your hands.
