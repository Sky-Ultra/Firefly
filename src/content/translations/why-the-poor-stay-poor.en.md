What can poor people do to stay poor? The secret is to have children indiscriminately.

The first question: why do poorer families tend to have children so indiscriminately? Some will say it is because the poor are playing a genetic lottery. Not at all; that explanation has not even reached the right stage. When poor people have children, they are not betting on the future. They are betting on the here and now.

Economics assumes that everyone is a rational actor who weighs the pros and cons under constraints and tries to choose what benefits them more. The "indiscriminate" part is the outcome, not the intention. The reason this kind of childbearing keeps happening is that it actually serves the initial interests of each person in a disadvantaged household, even though the outcome develops into people at the bottom harming one another.

So let me explain why having more children fits the rational logic of disadvantaged families so well.

First, a man and a woman become a married couple. Whether they genuinely want a child or contraception fails, their first child is born. At that point, the decision that best serves the family's interests is for the man to keep working, for the woman to keep working right up until delivery, and for both to share the housework equally.

If the man has a conscience, he should do a little more; if he does not, there is not much to be done. When it comes to housework, the interaction becomes one of the woman complaining and the man delaying, each trying to drag the other in, force the other to take part in the shared responsibility and labor, and find a way out for themselves. This is where the saying "a poor couple has a hundred sorrows" comes from, though it is also a form of strategic interaction.

After ten months of pregnancy, the woman delivers at full term. Ideally, both sets of parents can help care for the child. In practice, by that age, there is a good chance that at least one or two of the parents are seriously ill and bedridden, requiring the young family to devote physical effort and money to caring for them. You think the older generation is a constant, but in reality it is very much a variable.

At this point, the family's overall need is for someone to care for the child and possibly ill parents. The man's need is to save on expensive childcare; the woman's need is to recover properly after childbirth.

In this round, the family's decision is for the woman to stay home with the young infant and for the man to earn money alone. The downside is that the man shoulders the financial pressure. The upside is that the family stays intact: not divorcing means avoiding substantial child-support payments.

Meanwhile, as his working years accumulate, the man's human capital keeps appreciating. This is independent of his family circumstances; it is a long-term cultural asset belonging to him alone.

At the same time, once housework and parenting are successfully defined as the woman's duties, the man can stay out of them entirely. If asked, he says, "I've been exhausted all day at work. Can you please leave me alone now that I finally get to rest?" He also uses "You stay home without working; what is there to be tired about?" to continually devalue the woman's labor and weaken her bargaining power over it.

When the man pays all household expenses and the woman has no independent income, the allocation of that income becomes another round of bargaining.

The woman wants a fixed, adequate amount for living expenses every month, enough to ensure the child's healthy development and keep her own life from falling apart. She also wants to avoid the embarrassment of having to hold out her hand and ask for money each time.

The man wants to provide only basic living expenses each month and keep his earnings opaque, so that the woman cannot take a share of his property if the marriage breaks down.

Anyone who has raised children knows that, in this day and age, even if the state provides a very good safety net, and even if you do not intend to raise a child with meticulous care, bringing up a child costs a lot of money as long as you are not abusing them. When tuition is waived, there are miscellaneous school fees; when kindergarten fees are waived, there are nursery fees.

And think back to your own childhood: most of the time, it was your mother who took the lead in signing you up for extracurricular classes, and it was also your mother who paid whenever the fees came due. From the man's perspective, the preference is for low-cost child-rearing: keeping the child alive is enough. From the woman's perspective, however, she naturally takes on the emotional labor of parenting and is willing to spend more time and money with the child to give them a better future.

When the child reaches five to seven years old, they no longer need someone watching them every moment, so the woman's role as a caregiver is objectively diminished. In terms of the man's interests, he will want her to go out to work and help pay household expenses.

In terms of the woman's interests, the difficulty she faces in the job market after childbirth has multiplied. Why are mothers so enthusiastic about starting businesses, and so prone to being scammed? Because there really are not many jobs that let them care for children while making money. Behind every seemingly absurd decision, there must be a connection of interests you have failed to see.

When a woman bears the damage of childbirth and the permanent depreciation of her human capital alone, it is hardly surprising that she struggles to find a job that meets her needs for survival and dignity while also allowing her to look after the family.

So what does the woman do to escape social pressure and avoid returning to work?

First, she argues that the children and parents still need long-term, full-time care. Even if she goes back to work, the burdens of childcare and eldercare will not transfer elsewhere or disappear. She uses this in negotiations to postpone returning to work.

If that bargaining fails, she has another child and goes through another postpartum recovery period. She becomes the family's designated childbearer to avoid a possible "reassignment" or "malicious dismissal"—in other words, divorce. She uses children to bind the family together in exchange for stability.

As a result, more and more children are born. Although it looks as though the family's cash flow could collapse at any moment, do not forget that older children can become unpaid labor, babysitters, and tutors for the younger ones. Raising one sheep makes it a pet; raising a hundred makes it a food-processing plant.

At the same time, the children's futures become a portfolio for hedging risk. As long as one child finds work successfully and earns a reasonable income, they can support their parents and help shelter their siblings. Children also carry an obligation of filial support: the health coverage, workplace insurance, and social insurance the parents did not obtain through work are all shifted onto the children.

But after multiple births, a woman's outside options continue to shrink: she grows older, her health worsens, and her skills fall out of date. The more children she has, the less able she is to leave the family. Back pain, anemia, urinary leakage, pelvic-floor weakness, gynecological conditions, and other problems resulting from childbirth also arrive.

The man may appear less badly affected, but sustaining the household's income through prolonged, high-pressure work away from home, combined with unhealthy habits, may also leave the husband with conditions such as stomach cancer, lung cancer, diabetes, or high blood pressure.

If either spouse falls ill at this point and the family's insurance coverage is inadequate, informal borrowing can easily follow. When that money cannot be repaid, the debt becomes a new point of negotiation.

If the man tries to increase cash flow in the short term, he may go to an electronics factory to tighten screws, a construction site to move bricks, or a meat-processing plant to handle frozen goods. The risk of a workplace injury rises again. Once something happens, the family takes on additional, enormous medical debts.

At this point, the woman's dilemma is whether to use money intended for the children's education to combat the family's risks. If she does, the children miss educational opportunities, their chances of upward mobility decline, and the return on the family's bet on their future plunges.

She must also care for her severely injured husband. In other words, she has to look after three groups: her husband, the parents, and the children. This completely rules out her taking casual work outside the home.

Or should they stop trying to save someone who is beyond recovery? That would also mean losing the only source of income, and the whole family might not even survive to see the future.

So what can they do at that point? Keep having children. Have enough to become the number-one household on the targeted poverty-relief list, and let society provide a safety net. Keep going until the older children grow up, drop out of school, and work to supplement the household income.

And what is the prerequisite for having children indiscriminately? Marriage.

Sociology has a concept called the regional marriage squeeze, divided into a gender squeeze and a gradient squeeze.

A gender squeeze means that a particular region has more people of one gender—for example, more men than women—making it harder for men to find spouses.

A gradient squeeze means that women marry out to other villages, while men who marry women from elsewhere are likely to remain in their original villages. This leads to girls from the mountains marrying into lower-lying villages, girls from those villages marrying into the suburbs, and girls from the suburbs marrying into the city.

Over time, poor men at the bottom increasingly become indiscriminate about whom they marry. For instance, they may ignore a woman's actual circumstances—widowed, alone, disabled, or ill—as long as she can have children. Or they may mobilize the whole family's resources and take on debt to marry.

The woman's family is also unlikely to give up this hard-won opportunity to improve its circumstances, and starts demanding a hefty bride price. Everyone harms others in an effort to protect their own class interests.

After the marriage, they begin trying for children, because that was the marriage's original purpose.

Soon after marrying, the couple's life first acquires the illusory bubble of owning a home and a car, then develops serious disagreements over spending and ways of living. They belatedly realize that their productive capacity cannot keep up with their consumption. They begin fighting intensely and approach the brink of divorce.

Then having a child to improve the atmosphere at home also seems necessary. Everything described above follows naturally, and the timeline begins to converge here.
