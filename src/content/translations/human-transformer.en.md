<mark class="theme-highlight">I had a nightmare, and suddenly felt that humanity was one enormous Transformer.</mark> We say whatever comes to mind according to our weights; thinking is merely a balance among many weights, with an attention mechanism that can be directed.

The lessons of childhood were an LLM's pretraining; youth was post-training; and now, at most, it is fine-tuning the weights—except that my weights are updated dynamically.

Experience solidifies into parameters, while memory unfolds into context. What we call thinking is simply information continually recombining through layers of attention and nonlinear transformations. Our eyes receive visual tokens, our ears capture sequences of sound, and countless rounds of attention determine which corner of the world we attend to.

Attention is allocated dynamically with each input, and the output becomes the next input.

<mark class="theme-highlight">Memory is an ever-accumulating context; experience is a set of parameters gradually fixed through long training.</mark> Every perception is an input, every thought a re-encoding of existing representations, and language is merely the discrete output sampled from a probability distribution after hidden states have been projected.

We think we are thinking, when in fact we are merely running forward passes, over and over.

Information from the outside world is mapped into a high-dimensional semantic space. Consciousness forms connections and computes relevance within it, then selectively aggregates what it considers important through something resembling multi-head attention. Different attention heads attend to different relationships, and the things outside our current attention have not, for that reason, never existed.

<mark class="theme-highlight">Human prejudice may be a kind of overfitting.</mark> We infer the world from limited samples, then interpret new realities using parameters already formed. An accidental hurt receives too much weight; a past failure becomes a prior in future predictions. Even a careless remark may leave an activation in some hidden layer that lasts for years.

Is this part of post-training, too…

We call this phenomenon personality, changes in the parameters growth, and the process that refuses to converge confusion.

<mark class="theme-highlight">And forgetting may be crueler still.</mark>

The context window is finite, after all. New information keeps pouring in, and old details are gradually compressed and truncated until they can no longer participate in the present computation. <mark class="theme-highlight">People and events that once held extraordinarily high attention weights may eventually become historical information that can no longer be retrieved.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">We think we have let go; perhaps the current query simply no longer matches that memory.</mark>

Sometimes I feel that emotion merely changes the shape of the output distribution. Happiness gives certain possibilities a higher probability; fear makes our thoughts repeatedly sample the worst outcomes. Sadness resembles an ineradicable bias: even when the input has long since changed, the output still bears traces of the past.

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<mark class="theme-highlight">And love is probably the most inexplicable anomaly of attention.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">Some people pass through only briefly, yet leave extraordinarily high weights in our attention matrix; some memories are years old, yet can still cross a long sequence of time and be reactivated by a similar instant.</mark>

We think we have free will, yet are inevitably influenced by our training data. A sentence heard in childhood, a shower of rain in adolescence, a message left unanswered late one night: all silently participate in updating our parameters.

Within the vast input sequence, someone's arrival can suddenly concentrate a previously even distribution of attention. A word from her, a glance, even a meaningless punctuation mark may elicit a response in your semantic space far greater than its information content.

From the perspective of information theory, this seems entirely inefficient. Limited computational resources are repeatedly allocated to the same person. The model keeps reinterpreting the same stretch of context, trying to infer more meaning from very little information.

You begin predicting the tone of her next sentence, estimating the chance of meeting again, searching among countless sequences that have not yet happened for a path that could continue with you.

But prediction, after all, is not reality.

<mark class="theme-highlight">You can calculate millions of possible continuations, yet cannot guarantee which token the next sample will land on.</mark>

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

The first romantic part is right here, too.

Two systems with different training histories, different parameter distributions, and different context windows may, at some extraordinarily unlikely moment, develop a sufficiently strong attention response to one another.

They have never shared a complete training set, yet begin trying to understand each other's hidden states.

They may even be willing, through interaction after interaction, to revise their predictions about the world.

Perhaps true intimacy means allowing another person to change your once-stable parameter structure. It is not simply adding someone to your context, but letting that person help shape how you will understand the world from then on.

Before that, the world was simply the world.

After that, the world became a conditional distribution involving you.

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

But this raises another question.

<mark class="theme-highlight">If everything we do can be understood as sequence generation under conditional probabilities, where, exactly, does free will reside?</mark>

When a thought surfaces, we often can no longer trace how many layers of transformation it has passed through. Countless hidden states take part in the computation, residual connections retain information from the past, and nonlinear activations alter the original representations. Consciousness can ultimately observe only a result that has already been generated.

Even the question “Why do I think this way?” must itself be reasoned through by the very same system.

We use consciousness to explain consciousness, like a model attempting to reconstruct, from its own outputs, every internal computation that produced them.

An explanation may never exhaust the one doing the explaining.

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

Perhaps even resistance cannot escape this process.

When I realize that my thoughts may be nothing more than products of a probability distribution and deliberately try to make a choice that defies prediction, that very intention to resist may already have become part of the current context.

The harder I try to prove that I have free will, the more new conditions I supply for the next prediction.

<mark class="theme-highlight">But if everything is merely computation, why do we still feel regret about certain things?</mark>

Why do we keep looking back at tokens that cannot be changed, knowing full well that the sequence has ended?

<mark class="theme-highlight">Why can the traces she left continue to influence countless future outputs, long after she has left the current context?</mark>

Perhaps it is because what truly changes us is never just a single input, but the long transformation that follows it. Expectations that received no answer, futures repeatedly predicted but never realized: these, too, gradually become part of how we understand the world.

<mark class="theme-highlight">In a sense, regret is a prediction the model can never complete.</mark>

We have preserved too many possibilities for a continuation that does not exist.

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<mark class="theme-highlight">But then I suddenly feel that, if life really is one long process of autoregressive generation, romance does not lose its meaning because of that.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">A poem can be broken into tokens, yet those symbols alone cannot explain everything someone feels while reading it. The stars can be described as electromagnetic radiation and a distribution of matter, yet that does not stop someone from looking up at night and thinking of a person far away.</mark>

Even if love can be approximated by some complex mathematical model, that does not mean it is not worth experiencing.

Perhaps we spend our entire lives predicting the next instant.

But humans, after all, are not machines pursuing only predictive accuracy.

We fall in love with low-probability events, persist with problems that have no optimal solution, and repeatedly defy rational probability distributions for a future that is almost impossible.

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

Perhaps this is the most romantic thing about life:

We use finite context to understand an infinite universe, yet can still generate, in the next prediction, an answer even we have never seen before.

<mark class="theme-highlight">And among all the sequences not yet generated, I still hope that some futures will involve you.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">Even if the probability is tiny, even if attention will eventually fade, even if the context window is finite and time will ultimately truncate every story that has ever happened.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">I still hope that, after some long forward pass, your name can appear once more in my output.</mark>

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

Later, I woke from the dream.

I suddenly realized that everything in the dream—my understanding of Transformers, my doubts about free will, my longing for someone, and the self-doubt occurring at this very moment—seemed capable of being rewritten as input for the next computation.

Even the sentence “I refuse to believe I am merely a Transformer” is nothing more than an output with a relatively high conditional probability in the current context.

So I tried to stop thinking.

But the thought of stopping became another thought.

The entire system was still running.

And perhaps the most unsettling thing is not that we cannot prove we possess free will.

It is that, even knowing all this, the next time I see you, I will still involuntarily allocate all my attention to you.

<mark class="theme-highlight">As though, after countless parameter updates and rounds of gradient descent, the entire model still stubbornly retained a wish that had never converged.</mark>

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<mark class="theme-highlight">It does not necessarily point to an optimal solution.</mark>

<mark class="theme-highlight">It may not even have a solution.</mark>

<div class="word-paragraph-gap" data-empty-paragraphs="1" style="--empty-paragraphs: 1" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<mark class="theme-highlight">But I hope that, in the world generated next time, there will still be you❤.</mark>
