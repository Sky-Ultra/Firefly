## Introduction: Social Institutions Are Not Naturally Given

Many people consider a question:

Why have most civilizations in modern society developed systems of paternal surnames?

Why do children usually take their father's surname rather than their mother's?

Why has human history seen a transition from matrilineal kinship structures to patrilineal social structures?

More broadly, why are women gaining more and more social rights today while, at the same time, many ordinary men are also beginning to feel pressure from traditional gender structures?

On the surface, these questions concern relations between men and women. Behind them, however, lie deeper social dynamics:

**Kinship verification, modes of production, property inheritance, and changes in social structure.**

Many institutions were not simply designed by a single group. Rather, they gradually emerged under particular conditions of production to address practical problems.

---

## I. Why Did Paternal Surnames Become the Choice of Many Civilizations?

### Mothers Have a Naturally Certain Biological Relationship

In biological terms, a mother's relationship to her child possesses a natural certainty.

Women experience:

- Pregnancy;
- Childbirth;
- Nurturing.

Therefore, a mother can be one hundred percent certain:

> This child is of my own blood.

This certainty gives matrilineal kinship a naturally stable foundation.

---

### Why Does Paternal Kinship Need Confirmation Through Social Institutions?

By contrast, a father's biological relationship does not possess the same degree of certainty at the physiological level.

Men cannot directly establish through their own bodies:

> Whether this child necessarily shares my bloodline.

This is also why a series of social institutions gradually developed around paternal kinship as human societies evolved:

- Paternal surnames;
- Marriage;
- Clan systems;
- Rules of property inheritance.

One of the central functions of these institutions is to strengthen the social confirmation of paternal kinship.

In other words:

> Paternal relationships that cannot be fully confirmed physiologically need confirmation through social institutions.

---

## II. Surnames Are Not Only About Identity, but Also a System for Recognizing Kinship

Many people think of a surname as merely a name.

In traditional societies, however, surnames served more important functions:

### First, Confirming Family Membership

Sharing a surname signifies:

- Common ancestors;
- A common clan;
- Common relationships of inheritance.

In traditional agricultural societies, this was very important for passing on land, wealth, and power.

---

### Second, Helping Society Recognize Marital Relationships

If all relatives inherit surnames through the maternal line, some kinship relationships may be hidden within the system of shared surnames.

A paternal surname system, on the other hand, makes patrilineal clans clearer.

Ancestral halls and genealogies in traditional clans were not merely tools for wealth inheritance. They were also a system of social organization.

They addressed questions including:

- Who belongs to this family;
- Who is eligible to inherit;
- Which people cannot marry one another.

---

## III. Why Do Many Matrilineal Societies Have Weaker Marital Bonds?

One point must be made clear here:

A matrilineal society is not the same as a society ruled by women, nor do all matrilineal societies follow the same model of marriage.

However, anthropological research finds weaker marital bonds in some societies organized around matrilineal kinship.

The reason is not simply a matter of moral attitudes, but of the logic of kinship verification.

---

### When Paternity Cannot Be Confirmed, Patterns of Male Investment Change

In some matrilineal societies, the man who lives with a child over the long term and takes responsibility for raising that child is not necessarily the biological father. He may instead be the mother's brother: the maternal uncle.

The reason is that:

The maternal uncle can be certain:

> This child necessarily belongs to the bloodline of my own maternal family.

The biological father cannot attain the same degree of certainty.

Therefore, in this kind of social structure, matrilineal kinship is more stable.

---

### The Social Significance of Fatherhood Is Connected to Institutions

When a society cannot establish a stable mechanism for confirming paternal kinship, its institutional need for a paternal identity is naturally lower.

Once society enters a stage of accumulating private property, however, the situation begins to change.

---

## IV. How Did the Agricultural Revolution Encourage the Formation of Patrilineal Institutions?

Early human societies mainly relied on:

- Gathering;
- Hunting;
- Sharing resources within the group.

During this period, the accumulation of private wealth was limited.

The arrival of the agricultural era brought major changes to human societies.

---

### Land and Wealth Changed Social Dynamics

Agricultural production brought:

- Land;
- Livestock;
- Grain reserves;
- Private property.

As wealth began to accumulate, a new question arose:

> To whom should property be passed on?

A mother could, of course, identify her own children.

But if a man wanted to pass his wealth to his direct descendants, he needed to address the problem of verifying paternal kinship.

Thus:

```text
Private property emerges
        ↓
Heirs need to be identified
        ↓
Paternal kinship verification is strengthened
        ↓
Surname, marriage, and clan institutions develop
```

---

## V. Patriarchy Is Not Simply a Product of Male Will, but of the Structure of Production

Many social institutions cannot simply be understood as:

“One group oppressing another.”

More accurately:

Institutions are often shaped jointly by modes of production and practical needs.

During the era of agricultural civilization:

- Physical strength was an important productive force;
- Warfare depended on bodily strength;
- Developing land required large amounts of labor.

Because of the costs of childbirth and nurturing, women, on average, faced more constraints from family caregiving.

Men, on average, had an advantage in physical strength.

Thus:

```text
Agricultural production
+
Competition in physical strength
+
Inheritance of private property
=
Patrilineal institutions are strengthened
```

---

## VI. Why Is Modern Society Changing Traditional Gender Structures?

After the Industrial Revolution, human society entered a new stage.

Modern society increasingly depends on:

- Knowledge;
- Technology;
- Education;
- Management;
- Communication skills.

The core of productivity has gradually shifted from physical strength to intellectual ability.

This means that:

Some of the areas in which men previously held an advantage are diminishing.

Women have more and more opportunities to develop in fields including:

- Education;
- Business;
- Science and technology;
- Medicine;
- Management.

This is not simply the result of changing ideas. It is a result of changes in modes of production.

---

## VII. Women's Emancipation and Changes in Productive Forces

Engels once proposed:

> Women's emancipation is closely connected to changes in social modes of production.

The core logic of this view is that:

Gender relations in older societies were connected to private property, family structures, and systems of social hierarchy.

When modes of production change, older structures naturally face challenges.

In the past:

```text
Production based on physical strength
        ↓
Male advantage
        ↓
Traditional patriarchal structures
```

In modern times:

```text
Knowledge-based production
        ↓
Gender differences diminish
        ↓
New social relationships emerge
```

---

## VIII. Patriarchy Affects Not Only Women, but Also Ordinary Men

When discussing patriarchy, many people first think of the constraints imposed on women.

On the other hand, traditional patriarchal structures also establish extensive expectations of responsibility for men.

Especially for ordinary men, a patriarchal society offers expectations of responsibility more often than actual power.

---

### The First Layer of Pressure: You Must Succeed

Traditional ideas tell men:

- You must start a family;
- You must establish a career;
- You must take responsibility for your family.

Success seems to become an important proof of a man's worth.

In reality, however, not every man can obtain resources that match these responsibilities.

---

### The Second Layer of Pressure: Marriage Becomes a System of Social Evaluation

In traditional thinking:

Marriage is not only an emotional choice, but also a symbol of success.

Men are often expected to:

- Buy a home;
- Provide a stable income;
- Bear the family's financial responsibilities.

Marriage therefore becomes not only an emotional relationship, but also a form of social pressure.

---

### The Third Layer of Pressure: Responsibility Is Expanded Without Limit

Responsibility itself is not a problem.

The problem is that:

If a person must shoulder every responsibility without corresponding support or room to choose, responsibility can turn into pressure.

For ordinary men, traditional patriarchal structures sometimes mean:

> Having to play the role of the “family's pillar” without necessarily possessing actual social resources or power.

---

## IX. The Real Issue Is Not Men Versus Women, but the Pressures Left by Older Institutions

Patriarchy is not simply:

“Men benefit, and women suffer.”

It is more like a hierarchical structure:

- Women may face constraints imposed by their roles;
- Ordinary men may bear excessive responsibility;
- A small number of people who control resources gain more advantages.

Therefore, what truly needs reflection is not a particular gender, but:

> Whether a society can give everyone more freedom and greater equality in pursuing their development.

---

## X. From a Historical Perspective, Humanity Is Entering a New Stage

Looking back at human society as a whole:

### The Stage of Early Societies

Productive forces were limited:

- Wealth was scarce;
- Verifying kinship through the maternal line was more stable.

---

### The Stage of Agricultural Societies

Private property emerged:

- The need for inheritance increased;
- Patrilineal institutions developed;
- Clan structures were strengthened.

---

### The Stage of Industrial and Information Societies

Modes of production changed:

- Physical strength no longer determined all value;
- Differences between men's and women's capabilities narrowed further;
- Traditional gender structures began to adjust.

---

## Conclusion: Institutions Change as Productive Forces Change

Human history is not simply a history of antagonism between men and women.

More often, it is:

> A history of humanity continually adjusting social institutions to accommodate new modes of production and the needs of life.

From matrilineal kinship structures to patrilineal clan systems, and then to modern society's renewed exploration of gender relations.

What changes is not merely the status of men and women, but the entire way society is organized.

No one can fully predict where future society will go.

But one thing is certain:

As productive forces, economic structures, and human understanding continue to change, older institutions will continually be reconsidered.

History will not remain in the past.

It will continue searching for a new balance.
